# Pregnancy Food Safety Research

Use this skill when Clemente asks about foods, fish, contaminants, supplements, or diet-safety tradeoffs for pregnant people, people trying to conceive, or children.

## Setup

- Verify current guidance before giving numeric limits or lists. Prefer FDA/EPA, CDC, ACOG, state/local fish advisories, and peer-reviewed or government sources.
- Do not invent serving sizes, mercury thresholds, or species lists from memory. If sources are unavailable, say so and label guidance as needing verification.
- Keep medical boundaries clear: provide educational risk context and questions for a clinician; do not diagnose or replace prenatal care.

## Workflow

1. **Identify the situation**
   - Is the question for pregnancy, trying to conceive, breastfeeding, a child, or general adult risk?
   - Is it about store-bought seafood, recreationally caught fish, sushi/raw fish, canned tuna, supplements, or a one-time exposure?
   - Ask for species and amount when risk depends on dose.

2. **Check current authoritative guidance**
   - For fish mercury, verify the current FDA/EPA chart categories: best choices, good choices, and choices to avoid.
   - For locally caught fish, check state/local advisories; local contamination can override national guidance.
   - For pathogens or preparation questions, check CDC/FDA food-safety guidance for pregnancy.

3. **Explain the tradeoff**
   - Fish is usually encouraged in pregnancy because omega-3s, iodine, vitamin D, protein, and other nutrients support fetal development.
   - The goal is not “no fish”; it is choosing lower-mercury fish and avoiding high-mercury species.
   - Explain that methylmercury is the key concern because it can affect fetal nervous-system development, and it accumulates in larger, longer-lived predatory fish.

4. **Classify practical choices**
   - **Often preferred / lower mercury:** verify current examples such as salmon, sardines, anchovies, trout, tilapia, shrimp, pollock, cod, catfish, and canned light tuna.
   - **Limit / moderate mercury:** verify current examples and weekly limits, often including albacore/white tuna and some larger fish.
   - **Avoid in pregnancy:** verify current FDA/EPA avoid list before naming species; commonly includes large predatory fish such as shark, swordfish, king mackerel, tilefish, bigeye tuna, marlin, and orange roughy.
   - For raw/undercooked seafood, distinguish mercury risk from pathogen risk.

5. **Handle one-off exposures calmly**
   - For a single meal of a higher-mercury fish, avoid alarmism; risk is generally about cumulative exposure.
   - Suggest switching to low-mercury choices going forward and asking an OB/midwife if there was repeated high exposure or uncertainty.

6. **Give a concise answer with caveats**
   - State what is encouraged, what to limit, what to avoid, and when to check local advisories.
   - Include “ask your clinician” triggers: repeated high-mercury intake, symptoms of foodborne illness, immunocompromise, high-risk pregnancy, or local-caught fish without clear advisory data.

## Example: Mercury and fish in pregnancy

> The concern is mostly methylmercury, which builds up in large predatory fish and can affect fetal brain/nervous-system development. The usual guidance is not to avoid seafood entirely: choose low-mercury fish a couple of times per week, limit moderate-mercury fish like albacore tuna per current guidance, and avoid the high-mercury species on the current FDA/EPA list. For locally caught fish, check local advisories.
