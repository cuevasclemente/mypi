# Minimal Footwear Research Support

Use this skill when Clemente asks about minimalist/barefoot shoes, cushioned running shoes, tennis/basketball/court shoes, walking shoes, cycling footwear, shoe lifespan, or whether a named model is truly “minimal.”

## Setup

- Check Memoriki first for durable fit preferences and prior shoe history when relevant:
  - `~/src/memoriki/memoriki/wiki/`
  - MemPalace searches such as `shoe fit`, `minimalist shoes`, `Xero`, `wide toe box`, `tennis shoes`
- Use ExaSearch for current evidence and named products.
- Prefer authoritative/current sources:
  - systematic reviews and clinical/sports medicine sources (PubMed, Cochrane, BJSM, Scientific Reports)
  - manufacturer product pages for official marketing/specs
  - lab/spec sources such as RunRepeat when manufacturers omit stack height, drop, weight, width, or durability measurements
- Do not invent stack height, drop, weight, foam names, plate materials, price, or availability. If not verified, label it as unverified.
- This is research/support, not medical diagnosis. For pain, injury, diabetes/neuropathy, post-surgery feet, or persistent symptoms, recommend a clinician/podiatrist/physical therapist.

## Workflow

### 1. Clarify the use case

Ask only the missing questions needed to avoid a generic answer:

- Activity: walking, daily wear, running, lifting, tennis/pickleball, basketball, cycling, hiking, work/standing.
- Surface and intensity: pavement vs trail, hard court vs clay, lateral cuts vs straight-line use, loaded hiking, commute length.
- Current shoes and what works/doesn’t: width, toe pressure, heel slip, arch support, cushioning preference, pain points.
- Goal: comfort, foot strength, injury prevention, performance, durability, aesthetics, cost.
- Transition history: already wearing zero-drop/minimal shoes or coming from cushioned/supportive shoes.

### 2. Separate concepts that get conflated

Always distinguish:

- **Foot-shaped / wide toe box:** allows toe splay; often broadly useful even when not fully minimalist.
- **Zero drop:** heel and forefoot at the same height; changes calf/Achilles loading.
- **Low stack / thin sole:** increases ground feel but reduces underfoot protection.
- **High flexibility:** lets the foot bend but may reduce torsional/lateral support.
- **No motion-control structure:** not inherently better for every foot or injury history.
- **Advanced performance cushioning/carbon plates:** can improve running economy for some runners but is not “natural movement.”

Do not treat “minimalist,” “barefoot,” “natural,” “flexible,” and “low profile” as synonyms.

### 3. Review evidence with appropriate caution

For evidence-backed answers, search current literature. Useful queries:

- `minimalist shoes versus cushioned running shoes systematic review injury running economy`
- `running shoes preventing lower limb injuries Cochrane PubMed`
- `daily activity minimal footwear increases foot strength Scientific Reports`
- `minimalist footwear transition injury Achilles metatarsal stress fracture`
- `running shoe comfort filter preferred movement path Nigg`

Synthesize cautiously:

- Injury-prevention evidence across shoe categories is often low certainty; avoid saying any shoe type “prevents injuries” broadly.
- Minimal footwear can increase intrinsic foot demand/strength for some users, but load shifts toward calves, Achilles, plantar fascia, and metatarsals.
- Cushioning is not automatically protective; people adapt gait and loading.
- Comfort and individual biomechanics matter; a shoe that is good in aggregate may be wrong for a specific person.
- Activity context matters more than ideology.

### 4. Verify named products before describing specs

When Clemente names a shoe or you recommend one:

1. Fetch the manufacturer page for current positioning and official claims.
2. Fetch a spec/lab source if needed for stack, drop, weight, width, flexibility, outsole durability, or court-sport stability.
3. Note date/availability sensitivity for models that change yearly.
4. Quote only verified numbers.

Example source pattern from the source session:

- Nike Free RN NN: verify Nike page, then RunRepeat for measured heel/forefoot stack, drop, weight, flexibility, width.
- Nike Flex Experience: verify Nike page; treat “minimal” as Nike marketing unless specs show true barefoot traits.
- Xero court shoes: verify official Xero page for zero-drop/barefoot claims, outsole, insole/sole thickness, lateral support features.
- ASICS/Nike court shoes: verify official page for support/cushioning intent, then use lab reviews for measured stack/weight when official specs are missing.

### 5. Give activity-specific guidance

Use the task to decide how much minimalism is appropriate:

- **Daily walking / casual:** wide toe box and moderate flexibility often make sense; transition gradually if zero-drop/thin sole is new.
- **Running:** be conservative; distinguish performance economy from injury risk; recommend phased adaptation and no sudden mileage jump.
- **Lifting/gym:** stable low-drop shoes can help for lifting; plyometrics or mixed classes may need more cushioning/support.
- **Tennis/pickleball/basketball:** court-specific features matter: lateral containment, lockdown, outsole grip, toe drag durability, and torsional control. Minimalist court shoes can be reasonable for adapted users, but generic barefoot shoes are risky for hard lateral cuts.
- **Cycling:** stiff soles/pedal interface can matter more than barefoot feel; walking comfort and toe box still matter for touring or casual riding.
- **Hiking/rough terrain:** underfoot protection, traction, and load matter; thin soles may fatigue feet or increase bruising risk.
- **Long standing/occupational:** foot-shaped comfort can help, but very thin soles may be tiring on concrete.
- **Medical-risk feet:** avoid minimalist experimentation without clinical guidance.

### 6. Explain the cultural/market split neutrally

When discussing why footwear culture is fractured, include both sides:

- Mainstream brands optimize for immediate comfort, broad tolerance, high-margin cushioning tech, racing/performance narratives, liability reduction, and easy retail try-on.
- Minimalist brands emphasize foot strength, sensory feedback, toe splay, and lower interference, but marketing can overstate certainty.
- Big brands may offer “minimalist-inspired” lines (e.g. flexible, light, low-ish stack) without true zero-drop/anatomical/toe-splay designs.
- The evidence does not cleanly vindicate either ideology for all activities.

### 7. Recommend a transition protocol when relevant

If Clemente is moving toward minimal shoes:

- Start with walking and short daily wear before running or court sports.
- Increase time/mileage slowly; avoid changing shoe type, intensity, and volume all at once.
- Monitor calf/Achilles/plantar fascia/metatarsal soreness.
- Keep conventional shoes in rotation during transition.
- For court sports, start with drills or casual play before competitive lateral movement.

### 8. Record durable preferences

If the session establishes durable preferences, update Memoriki or MemPalace with uncertainty:

- width/toe-box preferences
- brands/models tried and fit outcomes
- activities Clemente wants minimalist shoes for
- injury/comfort constraints only with user consent and without sensitive medical detail beyond what is useful

## Output pattern

A strong answer usually includes:

1. **Short verdict:** e.g. “Nike Free is minimalist-inspired, not true barefoot.”
2. **Evidence summary:** what the literature can/cannot say.
3. **Spec table:** only verified product numbers.
4. **Activity-specific recommendation:** what to wear for walking/running/court/cycling.
5. **Transition cautions:** how to try safely.
6. **What would change the answer:** injury history, court surface, foot width, running volume, budget.

## Validation Checklist

- [ ] Memoriki checked when personal fit/history matters.
- [ ] Named products verified from current authoritative pages.
- [ ] Numeric specs sourced or explicitly omitted.
- [ ] Foot-shaped, zero-drop, stack height, flexibility, and support are not conflated.
- [ ] Evidence is presented with uncertainty, not ideology.
- [ ] Activity-specific load and movement patterns are considered.
- [ ] Transition risks are explained.

## Source-session techniques

- Used ExaSearch to verify systematic reviews and current product pages rather than relying on memory.
- Compared Cochrane/PubMed evidence on running-shoe injury prevention with studies on foot strength and running economy.
- Distinguished Nike’s “Free/Flex” minimalist-inspired products from true barefoot brands such as Xero, Vivobarefoot, Lems, Wildling, and Be Lenka.
