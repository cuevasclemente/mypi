# Motorcycle First-Bike Selection

Use this skill when Clemente asks about motorcycle categories, beginner bikes, first-bike recommendations, or motorcycle ownership tradeoffs. Pair with `motorcycle-risk-research` whenever the question involves overall danger, statistics, or risk comparisons.

## Setup

- Read `motorcycle-risk-research` if the answer should discuss crash risk, harm reduction, or comparisons with cars/bikes/transit.
- Search current authoritative sources before naming exact models or specs:
  - Manufacturer pages for weight, seat height, ABS availability, engine, MSRP, and model-year changes.
  - IIHS/HLDI or safety studies for ABS and class-level safety evidence when making safety claims.
  - Owner/manual/dealer pages only as secondary evidence for maintenance intervals and used-market caveats.
- Do not invent weights, seat heights, horsepower, prices, insurance costs, or model-year features.
- Treat this as decision support, not encouragement to ride. Safety-first tone is required.

## Core recommendation frame

For a first bike prioritizing safety, maintainability, practicality, highway ability, comfort, and cost, usually steer toward:

- Modern Japanese standard/naked, small ADV, mild sport-styled standard, or light cruiser.
- Roughly 300–500cc for most new riders; mild 650 twins only with strong caveats.
- ABS strongly preferred.
- Fuel injection preferred over carbureted project bikes.
- Manageable wet weight and seat height for the rider.
- Clean used bike with service history and good tires.

Avoid as first bikes:

- 600cc supersports and liter sportbikes.
- Large ADV/touring bikes.
- Big heavy cruisers.
- Old carbureted project bikes unless wrenching is the goal.
- Heavily modified, no-title, or neglected bikes.

## Workflow

### 1. Interview for fit and use case

Ask enough to avoid generic recommendations:

- Height/inseam and comfort with seat height.
- Riding context: city, highway, commuting, weekend rides, touring, gravel/dirt.
- Storage/parking/security constraints.
- Budget for bike plus insurance, gear, training, registration, and maintenance.
- Style preferences: standard, cruiser, sport, ADV, scooter, retro.
- Risk tolerance and whether the user has taken/will take an MSF/basic rider course.

### 2. Explain the major categories

Cover only as much detail as needed:

- **Standard/naked**: upright, versatile, easy to learn, limited wind protection.
- **Sport-styled beginner bikes**: small Ninja/R3/CBR-style bikes can be fine; supersports are not.
- **Cruisers**: low seat, approachable, but heavy cruisers and feet-forward controls can hinder learning.
- **Adventure/ADV**: practical and comfortable, but taller/heavier as size increases.
- **Dual-sports**: light and durable, but tall and less highway-comfortable.
- **Touring/sport-touring**: comfortable but usually too heavy/expensive for first bike.
- **Scooters/maxi-scooters**: highly practical; highway use requires larger maxi-scooter class.
- **Retro/scrambler**: style-forward standards; verify weight/maintenance.
- **Electric**: low routine maintenance but costly, range-limited, and torque can be beginner-unfriendly.

### 3. Build a tradeoff matrix

Discuss:

- Power vs forgiveness.
- Weight vs highway stability.
- Seat height vs confidence/control.
- Wind protection vs drop/repair cost.
- New vs used.
- Japanese maintainability vs European/American cost/character.
- Chain vs belt vs shaft.
- ABS, traction control, slipper/assist clutch, tires, and lighting.

### 4. Verify and shortlist models

For every exact model recommendation, verify current specs before stating them:

- Wet/curb weight.
- Seat height.
- ABS availability/standardness by model year.
- Fuel injection.
- Approximate MSRP/used-market note if sourced.
- Maintenance and dealer/parts availability.

Common categories to consider, after verification:

- Honda CB500F / CB500X / NX500 / SCL500 / Rebel 500.
- Kawasaki Z400 / Ninja 400 / Eliminator 450 / Versys-X 300.
- Yamaha MT-03 / R3.
- Suzuki SV650 only as a “mature beginner” option with ABS/model-year caveats.
- KTM 390 line only with maintenance/quirk caveats.
- Maxi-scooters such as Yamaha XMAX or Suzuki Burgman if practicality dominates.

Do not present these as fixed specs from memory.

### 5. Include total ownership and used-bike checks

A practical first-bike answer should include:

- Training course and licensing steps.
- Full-face helmet, gloves, jacket, pants, boots; budget before bike upgrades.
- Insurance quote before purchase.
- Tires: age and condition are safety-critical.
- Used-bike inspection: title/VIN, cold start, leaks, chain/sprockets, brake pads/fluid, fork seals, battery, service records, crash evidence, stock vs modified.
- Drop damage expectations for beginners; naked bikes are cheaper to drop than fully faired sportbikes.

### 6. Close with a ranked recommendation

Provide a concise ranked shortlist with “why it fits” and “why not,” plus a default if the user has no style preference.

Example:

> If you do not know what style you want yet, I would start by test-sitting verified ABS examples in the 300–500cc standard/small-ADV class. Pick the one that fits your body and budget best, not the one with the biggest engine.

## Safety baseline

Always include a short safety reality check:

- Motorcycles remain structurally much riskier than cars even with good choices.
- Risk reducers stack: ABS, modest power, training, full gear, good tires, sober/daylight/good-weather riding, route choice, emergency-braking practice.
- Do not imply any bike is “safe”; say “more forgiving” or “lower-risk choice.”

## Source-session techniques

- Built a category landscape before recommending models.
- Prioritized ABS, manageability, maintainability, comfort, and highway capability.
- Distinguished small beginner sport-styled bikes from true supersports.
- Recommended Japanese 300–500cc standards/small ADV/light cruisers as the default zone.
- Added safety reality check and harm-reduction stack rather than presenting motorcycles as broadly safe.
