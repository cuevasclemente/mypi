# Nutrition Food Composition Research

Use this skill for Clemente's general food-analysis questions: leafy greens, beans, squash, oatmeal pairings, plant milks, protein anchors, and whether a meal is nutritionally balanced. This is distinct from weight-loss timing/fasting advice and from medical nutrition therapy; keep the framing practical and non-diagnostic.

## Setup

- Check Memoriki first when the question depends on Clemente's preferences, diet pattern, or prior frameworks.
  - Clemente currently avoids mammals, but eats fish and chicken.
  - He values convenient, repeatable meal structures and often thinks in terms of nutrient density, satiety, and protein anchors.
- Use authoritative/current nutrition data when exact nutrient amounts matter:
  - USDA FoodData Central or official product labels for specific foods.
  - NIH Office of Dietary Supplements for micronutrient context.
  - National dietary guidelines or reputable clinical sources for broad recommendations.
- Do not invent precise nutrition numbers. If giving examples, label them as approximate and verify when the decision depends on them.
- Do not diagnose, prescribe therapeutic diets, or replace a clinician/dietitian.

## Core analytical lenses

### 1. Nutrient density vs calorie density

Separate at least three concepts:

- **Micronutrient density:** vitamins, minerals, carotenoids, folate, potassium, magnesium, etc. per calorie or per serving.
- **Calorie density:** how much energy a food contributes for a given volume/weight.
- **Meal role:** whether the food is a staple, protein anchor, vegetable/fiber component, fat/flavor source, or garnish.

Example: leafy greens are often macronutrient-light but can still be valuable because they bring micronutrients, volume, fiber, bitterness/freshness, and meal variety at low calorie cost. Their value is not only "replacing calories," though displacement can be part of the benefit.

### 2. Protein anchors vs hybrid staples

When Clemente asks whether a meal is "protein on carb" or "carb on carb," distinguish:

- **Lean/concentrated protein anchors:** chicken, fish, eggs, Greek yogurt, cottage cheese, tofu, tempeh, seitan, soy curls, many protein-enriched products.
- **Hybrid staples:** beans, lentils, chickpeas, peas, quinoa, whole grains. These provide meaningful protein but also substantial starch/fiber/carbohydrate.
- **Mostly carbohydrate bases:** rice, pasta, bread, oats, tortillas, potatoes.
- **Fat/flavor anchors:** olive oil, nuts, avocado, tahini, cheese in small amounts.

Beans are not "just carbs," but a bean taco is usually better described as a hybrid staple on a carb base unless paired with a stronger protein anchor or a large bean portion.

### 3. Fiber and satiety

Evaluate satiation through multiple mechanisms:

- protein adequacy
- fiber and resistant starch
- food volume and water content
- fat level and palatability
- chewing/texture
- meal timing and total energy needs

Avoid treating any one mechanism as universal. A bowl of greens may be filling for some meals but insufficient if the meal lacks protein, fat, or energy.

### 4. Culinary practicality

Clemente often wants easy structures, not abstract nutrient optimization. Suggest modular templates:

```text
protein anchor + vegetable/fiber component + carb/starch base + fat/flavor
```

Examples:

- chicken thigh + onions/mushrooms/celery/garlic + rice or beans + olive oil/sauce
- tofu/tempeh + greens + noodles/rice + peanut or sesame sauce
- sardines/salmon + greens + potatoes/grain + vinaigrette
- lentil/chickpea pasta + vegetable sauce + tofu, fish, chicken, or Greek yogurt-based sauce

## Workflow

### Step 1: Clarify the user's lens

Identify what Clemente is really asking:

- Is this food worth eating often?
- Is this a good protein source?
- Is this a vegetable, starch, or both?
- Is the meal balanced or missing something?
- Is the benefit micronutrients, satiety, calorie displacement, gut health, or convenience?

Answer the conceptual question first, then add examples.

### Step 2: Classify the food by role

Use practical categories rather than moral labels:

- leafy greens: low-energy vegetable/micronutrient and volume component
- zucchini/summer squash: watery, low-energy vegetable component
- kabocha/butternut/winter squash: starchy vegetable/carb component with carotenoids and fiber
- beans/lentils: hybrid carb + fiber + moderate protein staple
- tofu/tempeh/seitan/soy curls: plant protein anchors
- soy/pea-protein milk: plant milks that can contribute meaningful protein
- almond/oat/rice/coconut milk: usually not protein sources unless fortified/enriched

### Step 3: Verify nutrition data when precision matters

If Clemente asks for quantitative comparison, use current sources rather than memory. For example:

```text
USDA FoodData Central query: cooked black beans, cooked lentils, raw zucchini, raw butternut squash, cooked spinach, firm tofu, unsweetened soy milk
```

When exact brand matters, use the product label. Plant milks vary widely by brand and fortification.

### Step 4: Explain tradeoffs without overclaiming

Use language like:

- "I'd classify this as a hybrid staple rather than a lean protein."
- "Nutritionally closer to winter squash than zucchini."
- "Useful, but it may not carry the whole protein job unless the portion is large."
- "Fortified soy or pea-protein milk can do more work than oat/almond milk; check the label."
- "This is a good meal component, not necessarily a complete meal by itself."

Avoid implying one food is magic, detoxifying, or required.

### Step 5: Offer easy meal upgrades

Suggest simple changes that preserve the user's existing pattern.

For pasta:

- use chickpea/lentil pasta
- add tofu, tempeh, seitan, soy curls, fish, chicken, Greek yogurt, or cottage cheese
- add beans/lentils as a hybrid, not the only protein if high-protein is the goal

For oatmeal without dairy yogurt:

- cook or finish with unsweetened soy milk or pea-protein milk
- add soy yogurt if available and palatable
- pair with eggs, tofu scramble, tempeh, or leftover fish/chicken if savory is acceptable
- add nuts/seeds for fat/minerals, but do not mistake them for high-efficiency protein anchors
- use protein powder only if Clemente is comfortable with a supplement-like approach

For plant-forward meals:

- build around tofu, tempeh, seitan, soy curls, edamame, lentils/beans plus a stronger anchor when needed
- batch-prep protein anchors separately from vegetables/starches for convenience

## Example answer patterns

### Leafy greens

"Your basic conception is right: leafy greens are often micronutrient-dense relative to calories, but their value is broader than a vitamin checklist. They add volume, fiber, potassium/folate/carotenoids depending on the green, texture, bitterness/freshness, and can make richer meals feel more complete. They are usually not a protein or energy anchor, so I would treat them as a high-value meal component rather than the center of the meal."

### Beans as protein

"Beans are a hybrid. They bring meaningful protein, fiber, minerals, and slow carbs, so they are not 'just carbs.' But they are not protein-dense like chicken, fish, tofu, tempeh, seitan, eggs, or Greek yogurt. If the goal is a high-protein meal, beans may need either a larger portion or a companion protein anchor."

### Squash categories

"Zucchini is summer squash: harvested immature, watery, mild, low-energy. Butternut and kabocha are winter squash: mature, denser, starchier, sweeter, and more like a carb/starchy-vegetable component. Kabocha is nutritionally and culinarily closer to butternut than to zucchini."

### Oatmeal pairing

"A splash of milk improves taste and adds some nutrients, but unless the amount is substantial it may not turn oatmeal into a protein-balanced meal. For non-dairy options, soy milk or pea-protein milk are the default checks because they can contribute protein; most oat/almond/rice milks are mainly fluid plus carbs/fat unless specifically protein-enriched."

## Safety and boundaries

- For pregnancy, infant feeding, kidney disease, diabetes medication, eating disorder history, lipid disorders, or other medical contexts, use the relevant medical/food-safety skill and recommend clinician guidance.
- Be careful with supplement-like recommendations; verify ingredients and dose.
- Do not shame foods. Focus on roles, tradeoffs, and fit.
- If Clemente asks for a decision that depends on exact macros/micros, verify with current data and state uncertainty.
