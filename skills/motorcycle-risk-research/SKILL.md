# Motorcycle Risk Research

Use this skill when Clemente asks about motorcycle, scooter, moped, or powered-two-wheeler danger; comparisons with cars, bicycles, pedestrians, or transit; whether “responsible riding” closes the risk gap; or what gear/training/route choices actually reduce risk.

## Setup

- Check Memoriki first when the question references prior conversations or Clemente’s personal transportation preferences:
  - `~/src/memoriki/memoriki/wiki/`
  - MemPalace searches such as `motorcycle safety`, `motorcycle risk`, `vulnerable road users`, `transport risk`
- Use ExaSearch for current statistics and policy/equipment evidence.
- Prefer authoritative sources:
  - NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts and FARS/FHWA exposure data for U.S. fatality, injury, VMT, helmet, licensing, speeding, and alcohol statistics
  - IIHS/HLDI for ABS, motorcycle type, crash-loss, and equipment studies
  - European Road Safety Observatory (ERSO), IRTAD/OECD, Eurostat, national road-safety agencies for international comparisons
  - WHO Global Status Report on Road Safety for global/regional powered-two-wheeler context
  - peer-reviewed studies for specific interventions, always noting limitations
- Do not invent risk ratios, rates, or equipment effect sizes. Quote year, denominator, source, and population.
- This is risk research, not encouragement to ride. Keep tone nuanced and safety-first.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the comparison clearly

Before quoting numbers, determine:

- Vehicle class: motorcycle, scooter, moped, e-bike, bicycle, car, light truck, pedestrian.
- Geography and year: U.S. vs EU vs Japan vs global; current year vs older comparable datasets.
- Exposure denominator:
  - deaths per vehicle-mile/kilometer traveled
  - deaths per registered vehicle
  - deaths per rider/year
  - deaths per trip or per hour
  - per population burden
- Outcome: fatalities, serious injuries, all injuries, property damage, risk to rider vs risk imposed on others.
- Rider profile: recreational/highway riding, commuting, low-speed urban scooter use, touring, group rides, novice vs experienced.

State why denominators change the answer. Per-mile comparisons usually show structural risk; per-year risk depends heavily on how much someone rides.

### 2. Establish the baseline with current authoritative data

For U.S. baseline, useful searches:

- `NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts motorcycles 2023 fatality rate per 100 million vehicle miles`
- `NHTSA motorcycles alcohol impairment speeding helmet license 2023`
- `NHTSA Quick Facts 2023 motorcycles VMT registered vehicles`

When presenting baseline, include:

- motorcycle fatality rate per 100M VMT
- passenger car/light truck comparison per 100M VMT
- registered-vehicle rate if useful
- share of fatalities involving alcohol, no valid license, speeding, helmet nonuse, nighttime, single-vehicle crashes
- caveat that factors overlap and cannot be subtracted independently

Example pattern from source sessions:

> NHTSA 2023 reported motorcyclists at 31.39 deaths per 100M vehicle-miles vs passenger-car occupants at 1.13, about a 28× per-mile fatality gap. That is a baseline, not a personalized estimate.

### 3. Analyze confounders without false subtraction

Clemente often asks whether the raw danger gap is mostly thrill-seeking behavior. Handle this carefully:

- Identify behavioral factors: alcohol/drugs, speeding, unlicensed riding, helmet nonuse, nighttime riding, high-displacement/supersport bikes, group/rural curve riding.
- Identify structural factors: no crash cage, no seatbelt/airbags, conspicuity, single-track instability, road-surface vulnerability, high injury severity, intersection left-turn conflicts.
- Do not say “remove drunk riders and risk drops by X” unless a study directly estimates adjusted risk. Alcohol, licensing, helmet use, speed, and bike type overlap.
- A responsible rider can reduce risk materially, but cannot make a motorcycle as protective as a modern car.
- Mention that cars can also be made safer by defensive driving, route choice, speed choice, and vehicle safety tech, so compare best-practice to best-practice rather than best-case motorcycle to average car.

### 4. Compare vulnerable road-user modes fairly

When comparing motorcycles to pedestrians or bicycles:

- Pedestrians/cyclists share external-driver vulnerability, but motorcycles add high-speed operation, single-vehicle loss-of-control risk, and much longer motorized exposure distances.
- Bicycles and pedestrians often have different trip lengths; per-trip, per-mile, and per-hour rates can reverse intuitions.
- Motorcycles can also impose risk on passengers and others; usually less than cars by mass, but not zero.
- Road design and speed environment dominate vulnerable-user outcomes.

### 5. Separate motorcycle classes and contexts

Do not collapse all powered two-wheelers into one category:

- U.S.-style highway/recreational motorcycles: higher speeds, rural curves, large displacement, recreational exposure.
- Low-speed urban scooters/mopeds: lower kinetic energy, but traffic mixing, helmet compliance, and infrastructure matter.
- Southeast Asian mass scooter contexts: normalized presence may improve driver expectation, but population burden can be high because exposure is huge and emergency care/enforcement/helmet quality may vary.
- EU/Japan-style regulated contexts: stricter licensing, helmet norms, ABS, lower urban speeds, and driver expectation can reduce rates, but powered two-wheelers still tend to be many times riskier than cars per distance.
- Off-road/adventure riding: different injury mechanisms; road-fatality stats may not apply.

### 6. Evaluate interventions and harm reduction

For “what would make riding less dangerous,” verify current evidence and separate crash prevention from injury mitigation:

**Likely helpful, but source when quoting numbers:**

- DOT/ECE-compliant full-face helmet and protective gear.
- ABS and traction/control/stability aids where available.
- Formal training and licensing; refresher training for returning riders.
- Avoid alcohol/drugs entirely.
- Avoid nighttime, bad weather, rural high-speed curves, and high-speed/highway exposure.
- Choose smaller/lower-power bikes if appropriate.
- High-visibility gear and lighting; do not overclaim conspicuity benefits.
- Route planning: lower-speed roads, fewer left-turn conflict points, better pavement, less mixed high-speed traffic.
- Conservative riding: speed margin, lane positioning, escape paths, distance management.

Present residual risk plainly: these reduce risk, they do not erase the structural vulnerability.

### 7. Use international comparisons carefully

When Clemente asks whether the U.S. is unusually bad:

- Convert units correctly when needed (e.g. deaths per 100M miles to deaths per billion km).
- Prefer same-year/same-denominator comparisons; if using older IRTAD/ERSO tables, say they are older.
- Explain why some countries do better: lower speeds, more scooters than large motorcycles, training/licensing, helmet laws, ABS, infrastructure, driver expectation, emergency care.
- Do not infer that high motorcycle mode share automatically means low individual risk; population burden can remain high.

### 8. Preserve durable findings

If the session establishes reusable facts or Clemente preferences, record them in Memoriki/MemPalace:

- preferred framing for motorcycle risk decisions
- durable numeric facts with source/year
- Clemente’s risk tolerance or transportation constraints only if explicitly useful and not sensitive
- links to key reports/pages

Do not record speculative personal plans as facts.

## Output pattern

A strong answer usually includes:

1. **Bottom line:** e.g. “Responsible riding helps a lot, but does not close the car gap.”
2. **Baseline table:** rates and denominators with year/source.
3. **Confounder analysis:** what behavior explains vs what physics/road design explain.
4. **Scenario adjustment:** how a sober, trained, helmeted, ABS-equipped, daylight urban rider differs from the average.
5. **Comparison caveats:** pedestrians/bicycles/cars/international contexts.
6. **Harm-reduction list:** ranked by likely impact and feasibility.
7. **Uncertainties:** overlapping factors, data quality, denominator limits.

- [ ] Memoriki checked when prior-session context is requested.

- [ ] Denominator is explicit for every rate/ratio.

- [ ] Structural vulnerability and rider behavior are both addressed.
- [ ] Harm-reduction advice distinguishes crash prevention from injury mitigation.
- [ ] No invented numeric specs or risk ratios.

## Source-session techniques

- Used ExaSearch to find NHTSA Traffic Safety Facts for 2023 motorcycle fatality rates, VMT exposure, alcohol, helmet, and licensing data.
- Compared raw U.S. per-mile risk to international powered-two-wheeler context rather than assuming U.S. data generalized globally.
- Treated alcohol, no-license, helmet nonuse, and bike type as overlapping confounders, not independent filters to subtract from risk.
- Framed pedestrians/bicyclists/motorcyclists as vulnerable road users while explaining why motorcycles add speed and single-vehicle dynamics.
- Used WHO road-safety reports for global powered-two-wheeler burden and IIHS-style evidence for ABS/equipment patterns when available.
