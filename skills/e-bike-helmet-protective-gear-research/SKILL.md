# E-Bike Helmet & Protective-Gear Research

Use this skill for e-bike helmet, full-face, MIPS-like rotational-management feature, protective-clothing, glove, eye-protection, or footwear decisions. It supports safety-conscious choices; it does not replace local law, professional fitting, medical advice, training, or crash prevention.

## Setup and dependencies

- Check Memoriki first for voluntarily recorded bike fleet, prior gear, fit feedback, budget, and riding goals. Search narrowly; do not expose or repeat a home address, routine, precise routes, health details, or other sensitive data.
- Use current web research. Prefer, in order:
  1. official regulator/transport authority pages for e-bike classification and helmet rules;
  2. standards bodies or official certification-label guidance;
  3. manufacturer product pages, manuals, size charts, and warranty/recall pages;
  4. independent helmet-test organizations and peer-reviewed research;
  5. reputable retailer pages only for current availability/returns.
- Use ExaSearch/web fetch where available. No secret, login, or location data is required.
- Do not invent price, weight, certification, size range, ventilation, impact technology, replacement policy, stock, rating, or model-year detail.

## Privacy-aware requirements interview

Ask only what materially changes the recommendation. Offer coarse answers: city/region or country, not address; usual speed band, not GPS history.

1. **Jurisdiction:** country and state/province/city if rules can differ; rider age if legally relevant. Ask whether the bike is legally classified as an e-bike, moped, or something else there.
2. **Actual use:** assisted and downhill speed range, commute/utility/road/gravel/trail use, day/night, weather, traffic, cargo/passenger use, and frequency.
3. **Crash context:** high-speed traffic, intersections, descents, loose surfaces, off-road features, prior falls, or only low-speed separated paths. Do not ask for medical history unless the user volunteers it and it is necessary.
4. **Fit and comfort:** measured head circumference, head shape/pressure points, hair/eyewear/hearing aids, prescription glasses, desired sun/rain protection, heat tolerance, and whether a local shop fitting is possible.
5. **Gear needs:** abrasion exposure, cold/wet conditions, visibility, carrying load, pedals, and whether the goal is daily wearability or maximum coverage.
6. **Constraints:** budget, local try-on/return options, replacement availability, style, and any specific named product.

If key details are unknown, give a conditional default and identify what would change it rather than guessing.

## Research workflow

### 1. Establish legal and operating context

- Verify the jurisdiction’s **current** e-bike definition, speed/power classification, age rules, and helmet requirement from an official source. Terms such as “Class 3” are not universal; do not transfer rules across jurisdictions.
- Separate what is legally required from what is prudent for the rider’s actual speed and exposure.
- Assess risk by context, not label alone: speed, kinetic energy, traffic speed differential, intersections, road surface, visibility, weather, route design, load, rider experience, and emergency-braking skill all matter.
- State plainly that gear mitigates some injury mechanisms; it does not prevent a crash or make high-speed traffic exposure safe.

### 2. Verify helmet standards and labels

For every candidate, verify the exact model, market/region, size, and current certification claim from its manufacturer or a reliable certification source. A similar-looking model can differ by region or model year.

- Identify the relevant local baseline standard, often **CPSC** in the U.S. or **EN 1078** in many European markets; verify rather than assuming.
- For faster e-bike use, investigate whether the product is certified to an e-bike-specific standard such as **NTA 8776**, where applicable. Explain it as an additional standard/category, not proof that it is safe at every speed or in every crash.
- If relevant to the activity, verify additional claims such as **ASTM** downhill/BMX/mountain-bike certifications, **ECE 22.06** motorcycle certification, or other current regional standards. Do not conflate bicycle and motorcycle certifications or assume one legally substitutes for another.
- Ask the user to inspect the inside label and retain proof of purchase. Verify recall notices and manufacturer crash-replacement guidance before buying.
- Never say a certification, rotational-impact system, or test score “guarantees safety,” prevents concussion, or makes a helmet indestructible.

### 3. Use independent ratings correctly

- Look for an independent, transparent, current rating for the exact helmet version and size range, such as Virginia Tech Helmet Ratings when applicable.
- Record the rating organization, test type, date/version, exact model name, and whether the rating applies to the intended discipline. A rating is a comparative signal under its protocol, not a real-world injury prediction or a pass/fail legal certification.
- Treat MIPS and similar slip-plane/rotational-energy-management designs as product features to verify, not as a substitute for certification, fit, or an independent rating. Avoid unsupported claims about their effect size.
- If no comparable independent rating exists, say so. Prioritize verified certification, correct fit, reputable construction, seller/return policy, and transparent limitations rather than inventing a ranking.

### 4. Match coverage and retention to the ride

Use the least restrictive protection that the rider will wear correctly and consistently, while being honest when context justifies more coverage.

| Configuration | Often suitable for | Benefits | Tradeoffs / verification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Certified open-face bicycle helmet | Lower-speed, paved, everyday e-bike riding | lighter, cooler, easier to use consistently | limited chin/face coverage; verify local standard and fit |
| Extended-coverage e-bike/commuter helmet | Faster assisted riding or frequent urban traffic | may add coverage and an e-bike-specific certification | heat, weight, price, and exact certification vary |
| MTB trail/enduro helmet | Mixed-surface riding where its intended standard fits | rear/side coverage and visor options | not automatically suitable for high-speed road use; check certification and ventilation |
| Convertible or dedicated full-face MTB helmet | Technical trail/downhill or substantial face-impact exposure | chin-bar/face coverage | heavier/hotter; confirm the helmet’s applicable certification, retention design, and whether it is practical for the actual ride |
| Motorcycle helmet | Only when legal/use context and fit make it appropriate | robust coverage for its certified motorcycle context | can reduce hearing/awareness or cause heat/fatigue; bicycle ventilation and legal rules differ; never assume it is the right e-bike choice |

- Full-face is a coverage tradeoff, not an automatic upgrade. It can be sensible for technical descending or higher face-impact exposure, but heat stress, neck fatigue, reduced ventilation, communication, eyewear compatibility, and non-use can undermine the benefit.
- A visor can help with sun, rain, and debris but should not substitute for eye protection. Check line of sight and eyewear compatibility.
- Avoid attaching cameras, lights, horns, or aftermarket objects to a helmet unless the manufacturer explicitly permits it; attachments may affect snagging, shell integrity, warranty, or intended performance.

### 5. Fit and condition are non-negotiable

Give fitting steps, then recommend an in-person fit check when possible:

1. Measure head circumference at the manufacturer’s stated position and start with that brand’s current chart; do not convert sizes across brands by assumption.
2. Try the helmet level, low enough to protect the forehead, with the retention system adjusted as instructed.
3. It should be uniformly snug with no pressure point, and should not shift independently when the head moves. The chin strap should be secure and adjusted to the manufacturer guidance.
4. Test with usual glasses, hair, lights/earbuds if used, and riding posture. Check visibility, hearing, ventilation, and whether it remains comfortable long enough to be worn.
5. Do not buy a loose helmet hoping pads will solve fit; do not modify shell, liner, straps, or retention components.
6. Replace after a crash/impact when the manufacturer directs, if damaged/degraded, or at the manufacturer’s stated lifecycle. Do not rely on an undamaged appearance after an impact.

Do not diagnose head injury. After any crash with possible head impact or concerning symptoms, advise urgent medical evaluation/emergency services as appropriate.

### 6. Build a protective-gear system

Frame gear as layered risk reduction, tailored to heat and practical adherence:

- **Hands:** full-finger gloves can improve grip, weather comfort, and abrasion protection; verify palm/knuckle protection and dexterity for controls.
- **Eyes:** clear or low-light eye protection for debris/insects; appropriate sun lenses for daylight. Verify optical clarity and compatibility with prescription eyewear.
- **Abrasion/impact clothing:** durable cycling, MTB, moto, or workwear options vary greatly. For higher-speed pavement, assess garment construction, fit, heat burden, and any independently verified certification rather than trusting “protective” marketing. Knee/elbow/back/hip protection may trade mobility and heat for impact coverage.
- **Footwear:** secure, closed footwear with dependable pedal engagement and toe/ankle protection appropriate to pedal type and weather. Avoid loose laces or clothing near drivetrain/wheels.
- **Visibility/weather:** lights, reflectivity, bright/contrasting outer layers, rain layers, and thermal layers can improve conspicuity/comfort, but do not guarantee that drivers see the rider.
- **Bike-side protections:** tires, brakes, lighting, mirrors where appropriate, cargo security, maintenance, speed settings, route choice, training, and avoiding impairment are often as important as purchased body gear.

Avoid recommending bulky gear that makes braking, shoulder checks, pedaling, or heat management worse without discussing the tradeoff.

## Product verification and recommendation method

For each named or proposed product:

1. Confirm exact model, region, version, intended discipline, certification label/claim, current availability, size chart, warranty/crash replacement, and recall status from official sources.
2. Confirm an independent rating only if it matches the exact model and is current.
3. Identify fit trial and return conditions. Helmets must not be returned after real-world use or impact; verify retailer terms.
4. List evidence as **confirmed**, **seller-reported**, **user-reported**, or **unknown**. Do not infer unlisted protection from appearance or brand reputation.
5. Compare a small shortlist against the user’s actual use, fit, heat, budget, and replacement plan.

End with:

- **My pick:** a category or verified exact model, plus why it best fits the context.
- **Alternative:** the clearest coverage/comfort/price tradeoff.
- **Verify before purchase:** exact inside label, fit, current local rule, independent-rating match, recall/return policy.
- **What changes the answer:** higher real-world speed, technical terrain, hot weather, prescription eyewear, inability to achieve fit, or a jurisdictional change.

## Edge cases

- **E-bike modified beyond legal class / moped-like speed:** pause; verify classification, licensing, insurance, and helmet rules from official local sources. A bicycle recommendation may no longer apply.
- **Child/passenger:** verify local rules and child-specific fit; never scale an adult helmet recommendation down by assumption.
- **Cargo bike or trailer:** account for lower-speed but different fall/traffic/load risks; secure cargo and preserve visibility and control.
- **Shared, used, expired, or crash-history-unknown helmet:** recommend a new, traceable helmet unless provenance and condition are reliably known. Never recommend a visibly damaged or recalled helmet.
- **Heat, migraine, neck pain, sensory issues, or limited neck movement:** prioritize professional fitting and comfort; do not give medical clearance or prescribe a helmet type.
- **Off-road park/event rules:** check organizer rules and the exact discipline’s standards; a public-road requirement may differ.
- **No independent test exists:** do not substitute influencer rankings. Recommend verified certification, fit, a credible seller, and a conservative return/replacement plan.

## Examples

### Faster commuter e-bike

> “I ride a legally defined faster e-bike mainly on pavement and urban arterials.”

Verify local rule and actual speed first. Compare certified commuter/e-bike helmets that fit, then prioritize an exact-model independent rating if available. Recommend full-finger gloves, eye protection, lights, and clothing appropriate to weather/abrasion exposure. Explain that a full-face option is a comfort/coverage decision, not a universal requirement.

### Technical e-MTB descents

> “I climb assisted trails and descend technical terrain.”

Verify land-manager/event requirements and intended MTB certification. Compare trail versus verified full-face/convertible options by fit, heat, retention, and discipline-specific rating/certification. Add eye protection, gloves, knee/elbow protection as appropriate, while emphasizing speed management, skills, trail conditions, and not riding beyond ability.

### A specific helmet is named

> “Is [exact model] good for my Class 3 commute?”

Do not answer from memory. Confirm exact regional model, certification label, independent-rating listing, size chart, recall status, and return policy. Ask about actual speed and fit. If no evidence supports the requested use, say that clearly and suggest categories to try locally.

## Explicit limits

This skill cannot determine whether a particular helmet will prevent injury in a particular crash, certify a helmet, verify counterfeit products by photo alone, provide legal advice, or replace professional fitting, rider training, bicycle maintenance, emergency care, or clinician evaluation. Standards and regulations change; future agents must re-check current official sources before relying on any rule, certification, rating, specification, or recommendation.

## Source-session techniques

- Started from a Class 3 utility e-bike context and treated actual speed/exposure as more informative than a product category alone.
- Combined official-standard verification with independent helmet-rating research rather than treating either as sufficient on its own.
- Compared higher-speed e-bike standards, conventional bicycle standards, coverage, and comfort without claiming any standard guarantees protection.
