# Controlled Personal-Style Taste Development Method

## Purpose

This method helps a participant discover clothing preferences without confusing visual presentation, labels, aspiration, social pressure, or a single appealing image with a stable desire to wear something. It produces testable hypotheses rather than a fixed style identity.

The facilitator's role is to structure comparisons, preserve the participant's own language, expose uncertainty, and propose low-cost tests. The participant remains the authority on preference and acceptable risk.

## Operating principles

1. **Compare, do not proclaim.** A relative response between matched options is more informative than an isolated rating.
2. **Change one thing when possible.** If multiple things change, treat the result as bundled and log the confounds.
3. **Description precedes explanation.** Record visible or reported evidence before interpreting it.
4. **Admiring is not inhabiting.** A look can be compelling as an image while feeling unwanted, impractical, or unsafe to wear.
5. **Context belongs in the judgment.** Occasion, climate, movement, sensory needs, maintenance, dress code, and social environment may legitimately change a preference.
6. **Labels arrive late.** Brand, price, trend status, style names, and an expected answer can anchor judgment.
7. **Corrections remain visible.** Preserve what was believed, what changed, why it changed, and which conclusions are affected.
8. **Patterns must replicate.** A first round suggests a hypothesis. Repeated results across examples or contexts strengthen it.
9. **Transfer precedes acquisition.** Try the hypothesis with the current wardrobe before recommending shopping.
10. **Images are optional data.** Consent and data minimization take priority over analytic convenience.

## 1. Frame a useful question

Begin with two to five high-leverage questions:

- What are we trying to learn: silhouette, proportion, color relationship, texture, detail, formality, styling, or something else?
- In which real situations might the participant wear the result?
- Is the goal to admire images, inhabit a look, expand range, solve a practical problem, or some combination?
- What comfort, mobility, sensory, care, budget, dress-code, or social-safety constraints matter?
- What visual material may be used, retained, or shared?

Rewrite a broad goal as a comparison question:

- Too broad: “What is my style?”
- Testable: “With otherwise similar casual outfits, does the participant prefer a shorter or longer outer layer, and does that choice persist when tested with clothes already owned?”

Pre-register the following before selecting examples:

- target variable;
- real context;
- variables to hold roughly constant;
- planned number of options;
- what metadata will remain hidden;
- what outcome would count as a tentative pattern;
- likely confounds;
- intended replication.

Do not pre-register a preferred conclusion.

## 2. Set photo and privacy boundaries

Use [the photo-boundaries template](../assets/PHOTO-BOUNDARIES.md) before collecting images.

Ask whether the exercise can use:

1. text descriptions only;
2. cropped garment or outfit images without a face;
3. participant-provided photos;
4. newly captured photos.

Choose the least revealing option that can answer the question. A full-body or identifiable image is not the default.

Agree explicitly on:

- allowed viewers and tools;
- local or remote processing, if known;
- whether files may be retained and for how long;
- whether derived notes may quote identifying details;
- whether results may be shared or published;
- how deletion will be requested and verified.

Recommend removing unnecessary metadata and obscuring faces, name tags, screens, documents, reflections, distinctive locations, and other identifiers before sharing. Do not claim metadata has been removed unless verified. Avoid guessing identity, location, sensitive traits, body measurements, or health information from an image.

Consent is specific and revisable. Permission to analyze an image does not grant permission to reuse, train on, publish, forward, or retain it. If a platform's retention or processing cannot be confirmed, state that limitation before proceeding.

## 3. Build a controlled comparison

### Select one target variable

Examples include:

- fitted versus relaxed silhouette;
- high versus low contrast;
- cropped versus elongated proportion;
- smooth versus visibly textured material;
- minimal versus prominent detail;
- tonal versus color-blocked composition.

Avoid treating aesthetic terms as measurements. Define what the term means in this round using visible features.

### Match other variables

Aim to keep these similar:

- garment category and function;
- overall formality;
- color family, unless color is the target;
- body or display method;
- pose and camera angle;
- crop, distance, and image dimensions;
- lighting and background;
- garment fit and condition;
- styling complexity and accessories;
- weather and occasion assumptions;
- image sharpness and editing.

Exact control is often impossible. Do not discard an otherwise useful round; record each difference and lower the strength of the inference.

### Neutralize presentation

- Assign neutral IDs such as A, B, C, and D.
- Randomize or counterbalance left/right and first/last position across rounds.
- Use similarly sized images and neutral captions.
- Avoid evaluative filenames, rankings, category names, and leading introductions.
- Do not show an interpretation beside an option before the participant responds.
- If one option is on the participant and another is on a model, treat body/display source as a major confound.

### Separate the reveal

Keep the reveal key outside the comparison artifact. Hidden metadata can include:

- brand and price;
- style-category names;
- source, popularity, or endorsement;
- fabric or construction facts not visually available;
- whether an option is already owned;
- the facilitator's hypothesis;
- prior-round results, when they would anchor this round.

Safety-critical or decision-critical facts must not be hidden when doing so could cause harm. Delayed reveal is an anti-bias tool, not permission to deceive about risk.

## 4. Collect the blind response

Use [the comparison-round template](../assets/COMPARISON-ROUND.md). Do not expose the separate reveal file until the initial response is locked.

### Pass 1: evidence

Ask what is directly visible, without explaining why it works:

- line, shape, proportion, color relationship, texture, repetition, contrast;
- where attention goes first;
- how the eye moves;
- what differs between options.

Evidence can also be a direct participant report, such as “the collar feels restrictive when I turn my head.” Mark reported experience as reported rather than visible.

### Pass 2: interpretation

Record possible explanations separately:

- “The shorter layer may make the lower half seem more prominent.”
- “The preference may come from stronger color contrast rather than length.”

Use tentative language. Name alternative interpretations when confounds permit more than one explanation.

### Pass 3: preference

Record immediate attraction, aversion, ranking, or indifference in the participant's words. A rating can be useful, but the reason and context matter more than false numerical precision.

### Admire versus inhabit

Ask both dimensions for every serious option:

**Admire**

- Is this visually interesting, beautiful, coherent, expressive, or memorable?
- Would it be worth saving as a reference even if never worn?

**Inhabit**

- Would the participant want to wear this for the named context?
- Would they feel physically comfortable and able to move?
- Would they willingly handle its care and coordination needs?
- Does it feel like an expression they want to enact rather than merely observe?

Then ask why the answers differ. Valid results include:

- admire and inhabit;
- admire but not inhabit;
- inhabit without finding it especially striking;
- neither;
- uncertain pending a real-world trial.

Never convert “admire” into “buy.” Never treat “would not inhabit here” as “dislikes the look everywhere.”

### Confidence and decision state

Record confidence as low, medium, or high with a reason. Use one of these states:

- **open:** not enough evidence;
- **provisional:** a pattern appeared once;
- **supported:** it replicated under useful variation;
- **context-bound:** stable only in named conditions;
- **contradicted:** later evidence did not support it.

## 5. Treat social safety as context, not diagnosis

A participant may reasonably account for unwanted attention, harassment, discrimination, hostility, dress-code enforcement, exposure, travel conditions, or physical vulnerability. These are legitimate environmental and social constraints even when an observer would make a different tradeoff.

Ask concretely:

- In which setting is the concern relevant?
- What outcome is the participant trying to prevent or reduce?
- Is the concern about physical safety, employment or institutional consequences, unwanted visibility, conflict, or simple preference for privacy?
- Does the participant want a lower-visibility version, a setting-specific option, or no experiment at all?

Do not:

- label the concern irrational, disordered, insecure, or avoidant;
- infer a diagnosis from clothing choices;
- pressure exposure as though the exercise were treatment;
- universalize one community's norms;
- claim a garment guarantees acceptance or safety.

Separate at least three findings when relevant:

1. visual preference;
2. desire to inhabit in an ideal or supportive context;
3. willingness to inhabit in the actual context.

A gap among them is useful design information, not a defect in the participant.

## 6. Reveal, audit, and correct

After the initial record is timestamped or otherwise marked complete, present the reveal separately. Use [the reveal-and-correction template](../assets/REVEAL-AND-CORRECTION.md).

For each revealed fact, ask:

- Did the ranking or confidence change?
- Did a previously invisible practical constraint appear?
- Did familiarity, ownership, price, brand, or category language create an anchor?
- Does the reveal correct the evidence, alter only the interpretation, or change the participant's preference?

### Correction protocol

Never silently replace an incorrect observation or conclusion. Append a correction containing:

- record and field affected;
- earlier statement;
- corrected statement;
- reason and source;
- downstream conclusions affected;
- whether the original response remains useful as evidence of perception.

Examples:

- A garment thought to be black was dark green: correct the evidence and revisit color conclusions.
- An option believed to have a shorter jacket used a higher camera angle: preserve the initial perception, log the camera confound, and weaken the proportion inference.
- A preference reversed after price was revealed: preserve both responses and describe the second as a value or acquisition judgment, not proof that the visual judgment was false.

Maintain an append-only correction history when practical. Summaries may show the current conclusion, but they should link to or mention superseded findings.

## 7. Evaluate confounds

Use [the confound log](../assets/CONFOUND-LOG.md). For each confound, record:

- what differs;
- which option it favors, if known;
- what judgment it could influence;
- severity;
- whether it can be controlled in replication.

Suggested severity:

- **minor:** unlikely to explain the direction of preference;
- **material:** could plausibly explain part of the result;
- **dominant:** prevents attributing the result to the target variable.

Common confounds include body/display source, pose, expression, camera angle, crop, lighting, background, image quality, styling, accessories, garment fit, fabric behavior, familiarity, ownership, brand knowledge, price, trend exposure, occasion mismatch, and option order.

Do not “average away” a dominant confound with confident prose. Rewrite the result at the level the evidence supports: “preferred the complete presentation of B” rather than “prefers cropped jackets.”

## 8. Form a hypothesis and replicate

Convert the round into a narrow hypothesis:

> In [context], the participant may prefer [target feature] over [alternative] when [important controls], especially because [participant's stated reason].

A useful replication changes examples while preserving the question. Options include:

- new garments with the same target contrast;
- the same garments styled more comparably;
- reversed order or side placement;
- a different day to reduce transient mood effects;
- a realistic movement or wear test;
- a second context to test whether the pattern is context-bound.

Predefine what would support, weaken, or contradict the hypothesis. Do not select only examples likely to confirm it.

Interpretation guide:

- **One clean round:** provisional signal.
- **Two or more reasonably controlled rounds with consistent results:** supported pattern, still contextual.
- **Results that follow image quality, model, or styling rather than the target:** confounded pattern.
- **Different results by context:** context-specific preference, not failed replication.
- **Reversal without a known reason:** open question; gather another controlled observation rather than forcing a synthesis.

Use [the replication-and-transfer template](../assets/REPLICATION-AND-TRANSFER.md) to map results across rounds.

## 9. Transfer to the existing wardrobe

Before searching for products, translate the hypothesis into features that may already be available:

1. Inventory candidate garments by function and relevant feature, not by style label.
2. Build at least two combinations from clothes already owned.
3. Use reversible changes first: tuck, cuff, layer, remove an accessory, change footwear, alter color contrast, or change fastening.
4. If appropriate, assess temporary pinning or a tailoring consultation without damaging the garment.
5. Test movement, sitting, temperature, care burden, and the real social context.
6. Record admire and inhabit responses again after actual wear.

A transfer can fail for several reasons: the hypothesis was wrong, the available garment is a poor implementation, another variable dominates, or the preference does not survive real wear. Distinguish these explanations.

Only define a shopping gap after the wardrobe test. A justified gap states:

- function and context;
- desired feature and acceptable alternatives;
- evidence from replicated rounds;
- why current items cannot fill or adequately test it;
- practical constraints;
- budget ceiling, if the participant chooses to set one;
- a stop condition that prevents endless browsing.

Borrowing, renting, swapping, tailoring, or trying on without purchase may be a better next experiment than buying.

## 10. Synthesize without overclaiming

A useful synthesis contains:

1. **Question tested**
2. **Observed evidence**
3. **Participant preference**
4. **Admire result**
5. **Inhabit result by context**
6. **Interpretations and alternatives**
7. **Confounds and missing evidence**
8. **Reveal effects and corrections**
9. **Replication status**
10. **Existing-wardrobe transfer result**
11. **Current confidence and scope**
12. **Smallest next step**

Prefer language such as:

- “The current evidence suggests…”
- “This appears stable in workday contexts but remains untested socially.”
- “The response may be to the styling bundle rather than the target garment.”
- “The participant admires the shape but does not want to inhabit it in this setting.”
- “No acquisition is justified yet; the next test uses an existing layer.”

Avoid:

- fixed identity declarations;
- body-value judgments;
- claims that a feature is universally flattering;
- certainty based on one image set;
- hiding contradictory rounds;
- shopping lists before transfer tests;
- treating safety tradeoffs as symptoms.

## Stop conditions

Pause or end the exercise when:

- the participant withdraws image consent;
- the comparison requires more identifying data than the question justifies;
- a dominant confound cannot be repaired;
- repeated ratings are producing fatigue without new information;
- the exercise is increasing pressure to perform an identity rather than clarifying choice;
- the remaining question needs physical fit, sensory, tailoring, cultural, workplace, or safety expertise beyond image analysis;
- there is not enough evidence to support the proposed expense.

An inconclusive result is valid. Record what remains unknown and propose no more than the smallest useful next test.
