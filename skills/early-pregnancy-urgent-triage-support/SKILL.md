# Early Pregnancy Urgent Triage Support

## Setup
- This skill is for informational triage support only. Do not diagnose, reassure away red flags, or replace urgent care, an OB/GYN, midwife, or emergency services.
- Use when Clemente asks about early pregnancy complications, especially ectopic pregnancy, pregnancy of unknown location, pelvic/abdominal pain, shoulder-tip pain, dizziness/fainting, or vaginal bleeding.
- If the user describes current severe symptoms, prioritize escalation over explanation.
- If the question names a specific guideline, medication, test threshold, or clinical policy, verify current guidance with authoritative sources before giving detailed claims.

## Safety-first workflow

1. **Lead with the emergency boundary**
   - Say that ectopic pregnancy and significant early-pregnancy bleeding/pain can become life-threatening.
   - Ask whether this is about someone currently pregnant or possibly pregnant with symptoms.
   - For current red flags, advise ER/emergency services immediately rather than routine follow-up.

2. **Screen for red flags**
   Escalate immediately for any of:
   - Severe, worsening, or one-sided pelvic/abdominal pain
   - Shoulder-tip pain
   - Dizziness, fainting, weakness, pale/clammy feeling, or rapid heartbeat
   - Heavy vaginal bleeding or bleeding with significant pain
   - Rectal pressure, severe low-back pain, or signs of shock
   - Known positive pregnancy test plus significant pain/bleeding before an intrauterine pregnancy has been confirmed

3. **Explain typical timing without implying safety**
   - Ectopic pregnancy symptoms often begin around **4–8 weeks after the last menstrual period**, but timing varies.
   - Tubal rupture risk is often discussed around **6–10 weeks**, but rupture can happen earlier or later and is unpredictable.
   - A person can have mild or no symptoms before sudden worsening.

4. **Describe evaluation and prognosis plainly**
   - Evaluation commonly involves pregnancy testing, serial quantitative hCG, transvaginal ultrasound, vitals, blood count/type, and clinician exam.
   - Treatment may include methotrexate when stable and eligible, surgery when ruptured/unstable or not medication-eligible, or close expectant monitoring when hCG is falling and symptoms are mild under clinician direction.
   - With early diagnosis and treatment, prognosis is often good and fertility can be preserved, but future ectopic risk is higher and future pregnancies usually need early monitoring.

5. **Give practical next steps**
   - For red flags: “go to the ER now / call emergency services.”
   - For mild symptoms but possible pregnancy: “contact OB/GYN, midwife, urgent care, or an early pregnancy assessment service today.”
   - Encourage the person to bring: date of last menstrual period, pregnancy-test dates/results, bleeding amount, pain location/severity, prior ectopic/tubal surgery/PID/IUD/fertility treatment history, medications, and known hCG/ultrasound results.

6. **Maintain boundaries and tone**
   - Be calm and direct; do not catastrophize, but do not minimize.
   - Avoid definitive statements like “this is not ectopic” or “you can wait” unless based on clinician-confirmed information and still framed as non-diagnostic.
   - If symptoms are ambiguous, err toward medical evaluation because ectopic rupture is time-sensitive.

## Example response pattern

> Ectopic pregnancy can become serious before symptoms are dramatic, so if there is severe/worsening one-sided pelvic pain, shoulder-tip pain, fainting/dizziness, heavy bleeding, weakness, clamminess, or rapid heartbeat, treat it as an emergency and go to the ER/call emergency services now. Symptoms often appear around 4–8 weeks after the last menstrual period, and rupture risk is commonly a concern around 6–10 weeks, but it can happen unpredictably. If symptoms are mild but pregnancy is possible, contact an OB/GYN/urgent care today for hCG and ultrasound evaluation.

## Source-session techniques
- The source session answered an ectopic-pregnancy progression/prognosis question by:
  - defining ectopic pregnancy and its nonviability;
  - providing a timeline with uncertainty;
  - listing emergency red flags prominently;
  - explaining methotrexate, surgery, and monitoring at a high level;
  - ending with clear escalation guidance for current pain/bleeding.

## Do not
- Do not provide medication dosing or individualized eligibility for methotrexate.
- Do not interpret hCG trends or ultrasound findings as definitive without appropriate caveats.
- Do not ask for or store sensitive reproductive health details unless needed to answer the immediate question.
- Do not delay emergency escalation to gather more history when red flags are present.
