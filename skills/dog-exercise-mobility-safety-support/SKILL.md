# Dog Exercise and Mobility Safety Support

Use this skill for non-emergency dog mobility and exercise questions: running beside a bicycle, appropriate endurance work, helping an arthritic dog onto furniture, ramps/stairs, paw/heat concerns, conditioning, and home layout changes.

For urgent ingestion, collapse, severe pain, inability to walk, breathing trouble, or other acute symptoms, use `pet-urgent-triage-support` instead.

## Setup

- Give non-diagnostic, safety-first guidance; do not replace a veterinarian, rehab vet, or canine physical therapist.
- Ask for or use context: dog age, size, breed/body type, health conditions, medications, current activity level, surface/weather, and whether pain/limping is present.
- If images are provided, describe visible layout constraints and uncertainty. Do not overclaim medical findings from photos.
- Prefer low-risk modifications and gradual conditioning over maximal exercise.

## Workflow

1. **Classify the situation**
   - Exercise planning: biking, running, hiking, fetch, endurance.
   - Mobility support: sofa/bed/car access, stairs, slipping floors, arthritis, recovery.
   - Mixed: exercise for a senior dog or a dog with orthopedic limits.

2. **Screen for red flags**
   - New inability to stand/walk, severe or worsening lameness, yelping, neurologic signs, swollen abdomen, heatstroke signs, collapse, labored breathing, pale gums, or trauma → recommend prompt veterinary/ER contact.
   - For chronic arthritis changes, recommend vet follow-up for pain management and physical therapy planning.

3. **For biking/running with dogs**
   - State that it can be appropriate for some adult, healthy dogs when dog-paced and mostly a trot, but it is easy to do badly.
   - Good signs: loose lead/body, voluntary engagement, mostly trotting, normal recovery, cool weather, safe surface.
   - Bad signs: forced galloping, lagging, excessive panting, stumbling, paw soreness, heat, hard pavement, young puppies, brachycephalic/orthopedic-risk dogs, or increasing distance too fast.
   - Recommend gradual conditioning, short sessions, rest days, water breaks, paw checks, and stopping while the dog is still happy.
   - Avoid endorsing leash-in-hand cycling mechanics as safe; discuss inherent crash/entanglement risks and safer alternatives only at a high level.

4. **For arthritic/senior dog furniture access**
   - Prioritize reducing jumping down; down-jumps often stress shoulders, elbows, hips, and spine.
   - Prefer a long, wide, stable, grippy ramp when space allows.
   - Typical guidance for a sofa-height ramp: long enough for a gentle angle, often about 5-6 ft rather than short steep ramps; width about 16-20+ inches for medium/large dogs; carpet/rubber surface; no wobble/sliding; side edges/rails if anxious.
   - Use rugs, runners, non-slip mats, and furniture placement to create a straight approach.
   - Pet stairs may work for small/mobile dogs, but ramps are often better for large or arthritic dogs.

5. **Training and behavior**
   - Introduce ramps/stairs flat or low first, with treats and patience.
   - Never drag or force the dog onto equipment.
   - Block unsafe jumping routes while teaching the new route.
   - Reward slow controlled descent as much as ascent.

6. **Product or setup recommendations**
   - Verify current product specs if naming a product.
   - Focus on dimensions, grip, stability, weight rating, side rails, cleanability, and return policy.
   - For room-specific layouts, propose 1-3 placements and tradeoffs: best safety, least intrusive, easiest training.

## Example guidance snippets

### Bike exercise

"This is not inherently inappropriate, but the pace should be set by the dog. The dog should mostly trot, not gallop, and sessions should be short, cool-weather, and built up gradually. Stop if the dog lags, pants hard, stumbles, or has sore paws afterward."

### Sofa arthritis support

"The biggest win is stopping him from jumping down. For a larger arthritic dog, a long, wide, grippy ramp is usually better than steep pet stairs. Place it where he can approach straight from the rug, train with treats, and block the old jump path during the transition."

## Validation checklist

- Did you ask for dog-specific context when needed?
- Did you separate general safety advice from veterinary diagnosis?
- Did you screen for urgent red flags?
- Did you emphasize gradual conditioning and dog-paced exercise?
- For arthritis, did you prioritize preventing down-jumps and slipping?
- If using images, did you state layout-based observations without overclaiming medical facts?
