# Pet Urgent Triage Support

## Setup
- Use this when the user reports a pet emergency or near-emergency: swallowed bones/foreign objects, toxins, breathing trouble, bleeding, repeated vomiting, severe lethargy, pain, or uncertainty about whether to go to an emergency vet.
- You are not a veterinarian. Do **not** diagnose, prescribe, dose medications, or recommend home remedies as definitive treatment.
- If the user expects you to know the pet's size/breed/history, check Memoriki first when available, but keep urgent guidance moving while you look.
- Do not read secret files. Pet context may be in Memoriki/wiki or MemPalace; only summarize relevant facts.

## Core principles
1. **Triage first, explanation second.** Start with what to do now.
2. **Escalate red flags immediately.** If any red flag is present, tell the user to go to a local emergency vet now.
3. **Prefer local emergency veterinary care over online-only advice** when imaging, exam, decontamination, oxygen, fluids, or procedures might be needed.
4. **Avoid unsafe home interventions.** Do not advise inducing vomiting, giving oils/laxatives/medications, forcing food, or probing deeply in the mouth unless a vet specifically instructs the user.
5. **Stay calm and practical.** Users may be frightened; give a short checklist and a script for calls.

## Workflow

### 1. Rapidly identify the scenario
Ask or infer only the facts needed for immediate triage:
- Species, size/weight, age, breed if relevant.
- What happened and when.
- What may have been swallowed/exposed; amount and shape if known.
- Current behavior and symptoms.
- Whether a local emergency vet is reachable.

If the user already provided enough for action, do not stall with questions.

### 2. Screen for red flags
For dogs/cats with possible ingestion or trauma, red flags include:
- Trouble breathing, choking, collapse, pale/blue gums.
- Repeated gagging/retching, inability to swallow, heavy drooling, pawing at mouth.
- Repeated vomiting, blood in vomit/stool, black/tarry stool.
- Lethargy, weakness, severe restlessness, whining, hunched posture, belly pain, bloating.
- Seizures, tremors, disorientation.
- Straining or inability to urinate/defecate after a suspected obstruction risk.

If present: recommend going to an emergency vet immediately and calling ahead while en route.

### 3. For swallowed bones or sharp foreign objects
Use this pattern:
- State that acting normal is reassuring but does not fully rule out a swallowed shard/chunk.
- Recommend calling a local 24/7 emergency vet for triage; if no phone advice is available, consider checking in at the ER if the object was sharp, cooked, splintered, large, or the pet is small.
- Tell the user not to induce vomiting; sharp pieces can damage the esophagus coming back up.
- Avoid oil, laxatives, medications, bread/food remedies unless a vet instructs it.
- Save the object/photos in a bag and bring them to the vet.
- Monitor closely for 48–72 hours if a vet advises home monitoring.

Example response fragment:
> Because the bone looks splintered, call a 24/7 emergency vet now. If Vinny is breathing normally and acting normal, you can start with a triage call, but do not induce vomiting and do not give oil/laxatives/meds unless a vet tells you to. Save the bone/photos and go in immediately if vomiting, drooling, gagging, belly pain, lethargy, black/bloody stool, or straining appears.

### 4. Emergency vet vs online vet
- **Local emergency vet first** when the issue may need exam, X-ray, oxygen, antidote, surgery, or observation.
- **Online vet/tele-triage** can be useful when local ERs will not advise by phone, the pet is currently stable, or the user needs help deciding while calling around.
- Do not let online-vet steps delay emergency care if red flags appear.

Suggested script:
> My dog/cat may have swallowed [object/substance] at [time]. They currently [symptoms/normal]. They weigh about [weight]. I have photos/saved material. Should I monitor, come in for imaging/exam, or do anything specific tonight?

### 5. If the user reports barriers
If the ER has a long wait or will not advise by phone:
- Suggest calling 1–2 other local ERs if available.
- If red flags exist, go despite the wait.
- If the pet is fully normal and risk seems lower, online vet plus close monitoring may be a reasonable bridge, but present ER check-in as the safest option when sharp/unknown ingestion is plausible.

## Response style
- Use bullets and short steps.
- Be explicit about what **not** to do.
- Avoid certainty: use phrases like "more reassuring," "does not rule out," and "a vet may advise X-ray or monitoring."
- End with red flags and a clear next step.

## Validation before final answer
- Did you identify whether this is an immediate ER situation?
- Did you avoid diagnosis, dosing, or unsafe home remedies?
- Did you give a local emergency-vet path and an online-vet fallback only when appropriate?
- Did you include what information to prepare for the vet?
